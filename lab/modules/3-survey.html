Module 3. The survey reality

The NSCH-LC is not a simple random sample. Weights, strata and primary sampling units (PSUs) are load-bearing: they decide what the estimates mean and how uncertain they are.

The design, from the Census documentation

The Source and Accuracy Statement (pp. 6–7) specifies Taylor-series variance estimation with:

  • Weight FWC, the final child/young-adult longitudinal weight;
  • Strata original state FIPSSTO × STRATUM, with stratum “2A” recoded to “2” (the lab calls the combination STRATACROSS): strata;
  • PSU the household, HHID: PSUs, one sampled child each.

Weights: do it wrong, see what breaks

Unweighted, the sample shares reporting an ADHD diagnosis (ages 6–17) are % (English) and % (other-language). Weighted: % and %.

Here the weights move the rates by well under a percentage point. That is a finding about this file, not a licence: unweighted shares describe the respondents, weighted estimates describe the population the longitudinal weight targets: the 2018/2019 NSCH child cohort followed up in 2024, with a nonresponse adjustment and raking to the average of 2018 and 2019 American Community Survey child population estimates (Source and Accuracy Statement, “Final Weights Produced” and “Population Controls”). It is not a cross-section of all children in 2024.

Taylor linearisation in one paragraph

Every estimate here (a mean, a regression coefficient, a decomposition term) can be written as a weighted sum of per-child influence values. Sum those within each PSU, compare the PSU totals within each stratum, and add up the squared deviations, multiplied by nh/(nh−1). That is the with-replacement Taylor variance the Census documentation describes. For the decomposition, the influence of each child includes both its effect on the group means and its effect on the English coefficients (product rule), which is why the programs carry both.

Check yourself: the R program computes the descriptive SEs both by hand and with the survey package (Lumley, 2004); they agree to at least six decimals.

Domain estimation versus pre-subsetting

To estimate something for other-language children, keep the whole design and let children outside the domain contribute zeros. Pre-subsetting the file first discards PSUs from the variance calculation and can leave strata with a single PSU.

Falsification sandbox, survey cases

Why diagnose instead of being told: generating an answer, even a wrong one, before feedback is a desirable difficulty when feedback follows (Bjork & Bjork, 2020; Kornell, Hays & Bjork, 2009).

Singleton strata: two conventions

In the pre-subset file, strata have one PSU, which has no within-stratum deviation. Two common conventions:

  • Remove (a singleton contributes zero; R survey: options(survey.lonely.psu = "remove")): SE pp.
  • Adjust (centre the singleton at the grand mean, so it adds variance; "adjust"): SE pp.

Domain estimation on the full design needs neither convention: SE pp. The full NSCH-LC frame has no singleton strata; the lab's programs stop with an error if they ever find one.

Model n versus design-matrix n

The decomposition uses children. have every covariate, but of them have no ADHD answer. Report the model n; never “correct” it to the design-matrix n.

The PSU bootstrap: a second opinion on the variance

Resample households within each stratum (nh−1 draws with replacement, weights rescaled; Rao & Wu, 1988), refit, repeat 200 times (seed 20261117), and combine with the between-implicate variance (Rubin's rules). In this file each household contributed one child, so resampling households is the same as resampling children; what the bootstrap must respect here is the stratification. In a file with several children per household, resampling children one by one would ignore the clustering.

Compositional, not causal. A narrower interval is more precise, not more causal.

Six versions of family income

Income relative to the federal poverty level is multiply imputed by the Census Bureau (FPL_I1–FPL_I6). The programs run the whole decomposition six times, average the estimates, and add the between-implicate variance with Rubin's rules, as the Census guide to multiply imputed data describes. For the t critical value the programs use the Barnard–Rubin small-sample degrees of freedom (as the project pipeline did); with thousands of design df the critical value is essentially 1.96.
