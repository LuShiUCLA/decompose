Exercise A. The language gap in access to care

Question. Among children aged 6–17 in the NSCH-LC 2024 follow-up, how much of the English versus other-language gap in (i) a preventive visit in the past 12 months and (ii) having a usual source of sick care lines up with differences in demographics, family income and education, insurance and health need?

Program. programs/exercise-a/exercise_a_access_gap.py (or .R). It reuses Trap 1's structural-zero rule and Trap 2's CURRINS, runs an LPM Oaxaca–Blinder and a Fairlie (2005) logit decomposition side by side, and reports three specifications per outcome. The covariate choices are explained in data-notes/exercise_a_covariates.md.

Compositional, not causal. “Explained by insurance” means “lines up with the insurance difference”, not “insuring everyone would close it”.

The Fairlie columns use the English logit coefficients, the same reference as the LPM, so LPM versus Fairlie differs only by model. The pooled-logit column (one logit for both groups, no group indicator) changes the reference as well, which is why it can differ much more: compare it with Module 2’s reference switcher. Block contributions follow Fairlie (2005) in switching blocks one at a time, but pair children across groups by weighted quantile of predicted probability rather than by Fairlie’s random one-to-one matching, and average over every ordering of blocks instead of randomising it; the aggregate explained part does not depend on either choice. The Fairlie interval is a PSU bootstrap of the aggregate ( replicates); the traps-in rows are a falsification run and carry no interval. Specification B is fitted on exactly the children in A, so its gap is identical and only the covariate changes.

Why is n here () not the core study's ? Exercise A has a different outcome and its own covariate blocks, so a different set of children has every variable it needs. Each analysis uses its own complete cases; report n for each.

Can the explained part be larger than the gap? Yes. For preventive visits, Fairlie's explained part ( pp) is larger than the gap ( pp), so the unexplained part is negative ( pp). This means that, given their measured characteristics, other-language children would be expected to have an even lower rate than they do. A negative part is not an error. With intervals this wide, it is also compatible with an unexplained part of zero (the LPM's is pp).

Write it up

A model answer

“Among children aged 6–17, a preventive visit in the past year was percentage points (95% CI to ) more common in English-language households. Measured composition accounted for pp in the linear decomposition and pp in a Fairlie logit decomposition with the same (English) reference coefficients. Because having a usual source of care is itself an access outcome, we re-estimated without it on the same children: the explained part fell to pp (LPM) and pp (Fairlie). Block-level Fairlie contributions depend on the ordering of blocks; we report their range. The decomposition describes composition and does not identify causes.”

Exercise B. Care navigation, pooled annual NSCH 2016–2025

Question. Among publicly insured children with special health care needs whose care needed coordinating, is “effective navigation” (the family did not need extra help, or usually got as much as it wanted) less common in households that speak a language other than English, and how much of any gap is compositional?

What this exercise does and does not answer. “Publicly insured” means Medicaid, CHIP or another government plan: the public-use file does not separate Medicaid from the others. “Effective navigation” is this lab's own definition, built from the questionnaire's skip logic (definition). It is not the CAHMI indicator, whose documentation the lab did not have. If you have seen other figures for this topic (for example an analytic sample of 26,021 and a risk difference of −18.8 points), they come from a different analysis. They appear to compare outcomes by navigation status, not navigation by household language, but this could not be confirmed, so the lab does not use them. No risk ratio or E-value is reported here: the gap's interval includes zero, and when an interval includes the null, the E-value for the interval is 1 (VanderWeele & Ding, 2017), which adds nothing.

Why a different file. The coordination items (K5Q20_R, K5Q21, K5Q22) were not asked at the 2024 NSCH-LC follow-up (the Variable List marks them N for 2024), so this exercise pools ten annual NSCH files, 2016–2025.

Program. programs/exercise-b/exercise_b_care_navigation.py, then .R (the R program recomputes the sample flow, descriptives, both decompositions and every falsification run, and cross-checks each against the Python output). Definitions: data-notes/exercise_b_outcome.md; year-by-year codes: data-notes/exercise_b_harmonisation.md.

New skills. Pooling survey years (weight ÷ number of years; strata state × stratum; Census Guide to Multi-Year Estimates), a second decomposition family (DiNardo, Fortin & Lemieux, 1996, reweighting), and reading a small, imprecise result honestly.

Unexplained

Read it honestly. The gap is small, negative (other-language households report effective navigation slightly more often) and its interval includes zero. With only other-language children the study can rule out large gaps in either direction but not small ones: this is absence of evidence, not evidence of no gap. Decomposing a gap this uncertain tells you little about its parts.

Compositional, not causal. Neither family says why a gap exists; both re-weight or re-price measured characteristics.

A second family: DFL reweighting

Instead of a regression, DFL fits a logit of “other-language household” on the covariates and reweights English-language children by p/(1−p), so that their covariates look like the other-language group’s. Explained = English mean − reweighted English mean. Its weak point is overlap: here the reweighted English group has an effective sample size of only of , and one child carries % of the total weight. When few English children resemble the other-language group, the counterfactual rests on a handful of them; that is why its interval is wide.

How to read the period split. If the explained part were a stable feature of the population, it would be similar in both periods. Here it is negative in one period and positive in the other, and both intervals are wide and include zero. So the data cannot tell us even the direction of the explained part, and the pooled value should not be interpreted as a finding.

The explained part changes sign between periods (intervals overlap widely), and dropping a single year moves the gap anywhere from to pp. Both say the same thing as the wide intervals: the pooled estimate is not stable enough to carry a story about its parts. The weight revision moves the 2016–2018 explained part, so pooling original and revised files would mix two weighting systems; Census’s guide says to use only the enhanced files.

The skip trap. K5Q22 is asked only when the family wanted extra help. Treating its skip as missing keeps only those families: n falls to , effective navigation falls to % (English) and % (other-language), and the gap becomes pp. It is Trap 1 again, in a new file.

Write it up

A model answer

“Among publicly insured children with special health care needs who needed care coordination (NSCH 2016–2025, pooled), the difference in effective navigation, English-language minus other-language households, was percentage points (95% CI to ), compatible with no gap. Measured characteristics accounted for pp in an Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition and pp with DFL reweighting, both imprecise; DFL relied on few comparable English-language children (effective sample size ). Leaving out one survey year moved the gap between and pp. With other-language children, the data cannot exclude a small gap in either direction.”
